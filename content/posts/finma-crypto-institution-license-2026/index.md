The new licence categories come from the consultation paper the [Swiss Federal Council](https://www.admin.ch/gov/en/start.html) published in October 2025 — the proposal is the government's, not [FINMA](https://www.finma.ch/en/)'s, though FINMA would supervise the resulting licence holders.

If you want to run a crypto business in Switzerland today — an exchange desk, a custodial wallet, a crypto payment or transfer service — the licence most firms actually need is not a FINMA licence at all. It is **membership of a self-regulatory organisation (SRO)** recognised by FINMA under the Anti-Money Laundering Act (AMLA). That is what people usually mean by a "Swiss SRO licence." A FINMA banking or FinTech licence only comes into play once you take crypto deposits in a way that pools client assets, and a new FINMA-supervised *crypto-institution* licence is on the way but not yet law.

This field note walks through which route applies to you, how SRO admission works step by step, what the AMLA and KYC duties look like once you are in, what it costs, how long it takes — and then the 2026 reform that will move many SRO-supervised firms under direct FINMA supervision.

## Which Swiss Crypto License Do You Actually Need?

It depends on what you do with client assets. Read against FINMA's own [FinTech authorisation page](https://www.finma.ch/en/authorisation/fintech/), the routes sort roughly like this:

| What your business does | Route today | Supervised by |
|---|---|---|
| Exchanging or trading crypto for clients, custodial wallets, crypto transfers on behalf of others | SRO membership under AMLA | The SRO (which FINMA supervises) |
| Accepting crypto or fiat deposits from more than 20 clients in pooled (collective) custody | FinTech licence (up to CHF 100 million, no interest, no investing) or a banking licence | FINMA directly |
| Running a trading venue for DLT securities | DLT trading facility licence | FINMA directly |
| Custody, trading and staking of payment tokens *after* the FinIA reform | Proposed crypto-institution licence | FINMA directly (not yet in force) |

The FINMA page puts the core rule in one line: "If the business you do is subject to the AMLA, you must become a member of a self-regulatory organisation (SRO)." It lists trading with virtual currencies and providing wallet services among the activities that fall under AMLA. It also flags that a banking licence is not needed where crypto is held for each client on individual blockchain addresses rather than pooled — which is one reason so many Swiss crypto firms sit in the SRO tier rather than the FinTech or banking tier.

FINMA is explicit that "the authorisation requirements depend largely on the relevant circumstances in the case at hand." If your model sits on a boundary, its FinTech desk takes questions at fintech@finma.ch; that is the official route for a status enquiry, before you spend money on an SRO application.

## How to Join a Swiss SRO, Step by Step

The two largest general-purpose SROs, [VQF](https://www.vqf.ch/en/become-member) in Zug and [PolyReg](https://www.polyreg.ch/en/membership/frequently-asked-questions) in Zurich, publish their admission process. Put side by side, the path looks like this:

1. **Confirm your activity is within AMLA.** VQF, for one, offers no active membership without an AMLA-relevant activity. If you are unsure, ask FINMA first (see above).
2. **Set up the operating entity and the people.** For crypto applicants, PolyReg requires the contact person to be an operationally active member of the executive board who is resident in Switzerland. Every member has to appoint an AML officer with specialist knowledge; VQF says crypto applicants face higher requirements for that officer's experience and training.
3. **Write the compliance file.** PolyReg names what the substantive review looks at: a compliance concept, model contracts, general terms and conditions, and internal guidelines. AML officers submit a criminal record extract, ID copy, CV and qualification certificates.
4. **Pay the admission fee before review starts.** Both SROs only begin once the fee is paid. PolyReg keeps it even if the application is rejected — it is an examination fee.
5. **Formal check, then legal and compliance review.** VQF checks completeness first, requests anything missing, then reviews the business model in depth and may ask for more evidence.
6. **Admission interview.** VQF holds one for every applicant; PolyReg makes a face-to-face supervisory meeting mandatory for crypto (VASP) applicants.
7. **Written decision.** PolyReg's rule is the one to plan around: "The activity subject to regulation may only be commenced once written confirmation has been received."

After admission the obligations continue — training, audits and reporting, covered next.

## What AMLA Requires Once You Are In: KYC and the Other Duties

SRO membership is not a badge; it is a commitment to the due diligence duties in Articles 3 to 9 of the [Anti-Money Laundering Act](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/1998/892_892_892/en), enforced by your SRO's own regulations. In plain language:

- **Verify who your client is** (Art. 3) — identity documents for individuals, register extracts for companies.
- **Establish the beneficial owner** (Art. 4) — the natural person who ultimately owns or controls the assets, not just the account holder.
- **Repeat the checks when doubts arise** (Art. 5).
- **Clarify unusual transactions** (Art. 6) — if a transfer pattern or its economic purpose looks odd, you have to find out why and document it.
- **Keep records** (Art. 7) so an auditor can reconstruct what you checked.
- **Organise to prevent money laundering** (Art. 8) — internal directives, training, controls.
- **Report suspicions** (Art. 9) to MROS, the Money Laundering Reporting Office Switzerland.

Crypto adds two Swiss-specific layers. First, the travel rule: in its [Guidance 02/2019](https://finma.ch/en/news/2019/08/20190826-mm-kryptogwg/), FINMA said that information about the client and the beneficiary must travel with token transfers, and that Swiss institutions may only send tokens to, or receive them from, external wallets belonging to their own verified customers as long as that data cannot be transmitted reliably. FINMA described this as stricter than the FATF standard, because it applies without the exception for unregulated wallets. Second, the SRO's own supervision: VQF requires a full day of AMLA basic training within twelve months of joining and a half-day refresher every two years, and audits members every 12, 24 or 36 months depending on risk. PolyReg inspects members on average once a year, never defers the initial audit, and grants no audit deferral to crypto members.

## How Much Does a Crypto License Cost in Switzerland?

For the SRO route, the published fees are modest; the real cost is people and audits. PolyReg is the one SRO that publishes a full fee table on its [costs page](https://www.polyreg.ch/en/membership/costs):

| PolyReg company size | Admission fee | Annual fee |
|---|---|---|
| I (1–3 persons) | CHF 1,600 | CHF 1,800 |
| II (4–8 persons) | CHF 2,600 | CHF 3,000 |
| III (9–27 persons) | CHF 5,000 | CHF 5,800 |
| IV (28+ persons) | from CHF 12,000 | from CHF 15,000 |

Those are the full rates; reduced rates apply to firms whose activity is not mainly AMLA-relevant, which is rarely the case for a crypto business. On top of that, PolyReg charges additional review fees for VASP applications, which its FAQ mentions without stating an amount. VQF charges according to a separate fee regulation that its admission page refers to but does not reproduce, so ask for the current version before you apply.

Three costs are not published anywhere I could find, and they are usually the larger ones: the external audit firm that carries out the annual or periodic SRO audit, the salary or retainer of a qualified AML officer, and legal help drafting the compliance file. For the FINMA routes, the FinTech licence page says only that "the authorisation process is subject to fees" set by FINMA's Fees and Levies Ordinance, without amounts. Any total "crypto license cost" figure you see quoted as a single number is a provider's package price, not an official one.

## How Long Does SRO Admission Take?

PolyReg gives the only published range: a decision "can be reached within a period of 4 to 12 weeks" after a complete application — and it adds that VASP and complex applications are likely to take longer. VQF gives no fixed timeframe; it says processing depends on the application being complete and correct. Either way, the clock starts when the fee is paid and the file is complete, and you cannot start the regulated activity until the written confirmation arrives. Build that wait into any launch plan.

## The 2026 Change: Two New FINMA License Categories

On October 22, 2025, the [Swiss Federal Council](https://www.admin.ch/gov/en/start.html) released a draft amendment to the Financial Institutions Act — FinIA — and opened a public consultation. The document proposes two new license categories: the *crypto-institution* (Krypto-Institut) and the *payment instrument institution* (Zahlungsmittelinstitut). The consultation period closed on February 6, 2026.

When [FINMA issued its first banking licenses to [SEBA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SEBA_Bank) and [Sygnum](https://www.sygnum.com/) in 2019](/posts/finma-crypto-license-2019/), Switzerland signaled it was serious about institutional crypto infrastructure. This proposal feels like the next structural layer: not just licensing a handful of elite firms, but building a taxonomy for the entire sector — and, for anyone planning around an SRO membership today, it is the reason that membership may be a transition step rather than the end state.

Here is what I have pieced together from reading the consultation document and the legal commentary around it.

### The Problem the Proposal Is Trying to Solve

The current situation is messier than the headline picture suggests. At headline level, Switzerland's crypto landscape looks orderly: FINMA, the [DLT Act](https://www.sif.admin.ch/en/dlt-blockchain-en), clear rules, respectable institutions. Look at which licences firms actually hold, and the picture is more fragmented.

Crypto firms operating in Switzerland today can be in one of several regulatory buckets. Some hold banking licenses — like SEBA (now AMINA) and Sygnum. A much larger group operates under self-regulatory organization (SRO) supervision purely for AML/CFT compliance, with limited formal FINMA oversight over their core activities. A smaller number hold the fintech license introduced in 2019, which capped deposits at CHF 100 million and never quite fit what most crypto businesses actually do.

The result is that the industry has been running on a patchwork. FINMA has been issuing guidance — most recently the [custody guidance from January 2026](/posts/finma-crypto-guidance-01-2026/) — to fill gaps within that existing framework. But guidance has limits. You cannot patch a structural mismatch with circulars.

The FinIA amendment is the structural fix.

### What the Crypto-Institution License Actually Covers

The crypto-institution license is designed for companies providing custody and trading of crypto-based assets with trading characteristics. In practice, that means:

- Custody of payment tokens such as [Bitcoin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitcoin) and Ether
- Provision of [staking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof_of_stake) services
- Client brokerage and trading services
- Short-term proprietary trading and exchange operations
- Custody of foreign [stablecoin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stablecoin)s

What it explicitly does *not* cover: pure utility tokens, central bank digital currencies (CBDCs), and securities tokens. Those remain in separate regulatory territory.

Think of the firms that currently operate under SRO supervision — exchanges, custodians, brokers that are not banks. The crypto-institution license is built for them. It provides a formal regulatory home that is lighter than a full securities firm license, but requires direct FINMA oversight rather than just SRO registration.
{{< /sidenote >}}

The regulatory model is explicitly described as lighter than the framework applicable to securities firms. That framing matters. Switzerland already has securities dealer licenses for firms that want full capital markets access. The crypto-institution license is not that. It is calibrated for the specific risk profile of crypto custody and trading — which involves market risk, cybersecurity risk, and custody risk, but not the credit risk or leverage risk that comes with securities dealing.

On prohibited activities: crypto institutions cannot engage in uncovered proprietary trading and cannot lend client assets. This is a meaningful constraint that separates them from banks and securities dealers who can do both.

### What the Payment Instrument Institution License Covers

The payment instrument institution is the second new category, and in some ways the more technically interesting one because it replaces the existing fintech license.

The current fintech license was introduced under Article 1b of the Banking Act and [entered into force on January 1, 2019](https://www.finma.ch/en/authorisation/fintech/fintech-bewilligung/). It is a distinct track from the [DLT Act](/posts/dlt-act-switzerland-explained/), which came later — the two are often lumped together as "Switzerland's crypto rules," but the fintech license is a banking-law instrument, not part of the distributed-ledger package. It let firms accept public deposits up to CHF 100 million without lending against them. That ceiling has been the binding constraint for fintech-scale payment businesses that wanted to grow without converting to a full banking license. The new payment instrument institution category removes that cap.

The specific rights granted under this license include:

**Unlimited client fund acceptance** — funds held on behalf of clients, without lending or paying interest. Client money must remain segregated from the firm's own assets and protected in the event of insolvency.

**Exclusive stablecoin issuance rights** — this is the most notable new feature. Payment instrument institutions gain the exclusive right to issue CHF-pegged stablecoins that are fully backed by equivalent assets and redeemable at par on demand. The backing assets must meet quality standards, and firms must publish a whitepaper describing token terms, reserves, and associated risks.

One notable structural requirement: traditional banks cannot simply add stablecoin issuance to their existing license. To issue CHF-pegged stablecoins under this framework, a bank would need to establish a separate subsidiary that holds the payment instrument institution license. The activities cannot be combined with traditional banking functions.

### How This Compares to the Existing License Landscape

Switzerland's pre-reform crypto licensing structure had three main paths:

| License Type | What It Permitted | Key Constraint |
|---|---|---|
| Banking license | Deposits, custody, lending, securities | Very high capital requirements, full FINMA regime |
| Securities dealer license | Trading, brokerage, client execution | Strong capital and compliance requirements |
| Fintech license | Client funds up to CHF 100M, no lending | CHF 100M deposit cap — the ceiling that constrained growth |
| SRO membership | AML/CFT supervision only | No formal regulation of business activities, no direct FINMA oversight |

The new structure adds two purpose-built categories below the banking license tier, with direct FINMA supervision:

| New Category | Target Activities | Key Feature |
|---|---|---|
| Payment Instrument Institution | Payments, client funds, stablecoin issuance | Removes CHF 100M cap; stablecoin exclusivity |
| Crypto-Institution | Custody, trading, staking, exchange | Lighter than securities firm; no lending/credit |

For firms currently under SRO supervision, this is the significant shift. SRO oversight covers AML compliance, but it does not subject firms to FINMA's ongoing supervision of their business conduct, governance, or capital adequacy. The crypto-institution license changes that. Once this framework is in force, custodians and exchanges that previously operated under SRO-only oversight will need to obtain a crypto-institution license and enter direct FINMA supervision — or stop operating.

### How It Maps onto EU [MiCA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markets_in_Crypto-Assets)

Comparing Swiss crypto regulation to the EU's Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation (MiCA) is something I covered more systematically [in the MiCA vs FINMA field note](/posts/mica-vs-finma-field-note/). The short version of how the FinIA amendment relates to MiCA:

The Federal Council's consultation paper explicitly frames the reform as aligning with international standards — specifically the Financial Stability Board's framework and MiCA. The stablecoin rules are the clearest example of that alignment: fully backed, segregated, redeemable at par, whitepaper disclosure. That is essentially the MiCA e-money token (EMT) model applied at a national level.

On the crypto service provider side, MiCA's Crypto-Asset Service Provider (CASP) authorization is roughly analogous to the Swiss crypto-institution license — both create a regulated tier below full banking authorization, with custody and trading as the core permitted activities.

The differences are in the details. MiCA applies EU-wide passporting, which Swiss firms cannot access. The Swiss crypto-institution license is a domestic authorization only. A Swiss firm that wants to serve EU clients at scale still needs to either obtain a MiCA authorization in an EU member state or rely on reverse solicitation rules.

What the Swiss framework gains in return: it is not bound by MiCA's one-size-fits-all design. The Federal Council can calibrate requirements to Swiss market conditions and risk profiles rather than harmonizing to the median EU case.

### What the Timeline Actually Looks Like

The consultation closed February 6, 2026. The next steps follow Switzerland's standard legislative process:

1. The Federal Council reviews consultation responses and prepares a final draft
2. Parliament debates and votes on the FinIA amendment
3. Once passed, an implementation period allows existing firms to transition

The earliest realistic entry into force is late 2027. For firms currently under SRO supervision, the transition rules matter: the proposal suggests that firms affiliated with a recognized SRO can continue operating during the transition period while they apply for the appropriate new license.

That transition window is not unlimited. The practical planning horizon for affected firms is now.

### What Changes in Practice — and What Does Not

After reading the consultation paper and the commentary around it, a few things stand out as practically significant:

**For SRO-supervised exchanges and custodians:** The shift to direct FINMA oversight is real. Governance formalization, compliance functions, external audits, annual reporting — these requirements exist at banks already, but they are new costs for firms that have operated under lighter SRO supervision. Smaller operators may find the compliance burden prohibitive.

**For payment-focused fintech firms:** The removal of the CHF 100 million cap is genuinely useful. Firms that have been constrained by that ceiling can now plan for growth without needing to convert to a full banking license.

**For stablecoin issuers:** The [CHF stablecoin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stablecoin) exclusivity granted to payment instrument institutions creates a clear legal path. Currently, CHF-pegged stablecoins exist in a grey area. This would define what a legally sound CHF stablecoin looks like and who can issue it.

**For banks:** The requirement that banks spin off a separate subsidiary to participate in stablecoin issuance is worth noting. It is not a prohibition — it is a structural constraint that reflects the policy view that banks should not mix traditional deposit functions with stablecoin issuance.

What does not change immediately: the underlying regulatory philosophy. Switzerland is still calibrating — not prohibiting. The direction continues to be toward formalization of a sector that exists, rather than attempts to suppress or redirect it.

### A Word on Capital Requirements

One noticeable gap in the consultation paper, at least in the version that has been publicly discussed: specific numerical capital requirements for the crypto-institution license have not been disclosed. The proposal references "adequate capital" and "progressive capital requirements" for significant payment instrument institutions, but no CHF thresholds have been specified in the commentary I have seen.

This will matter significantly once the legislation moves toward parliamentary drafting. The capital level is what determines whether the crypto-institution license is genuinely accessible to mid-sized Swiss crypto firms, or whether it becomes another regime that only well-capitalized players can realistically obtain. The outcome of that calibration is still open.

## Is Cryptocurrency Allowed in Switzerland?

Yes. Owning, buying and selling crypto is legal, and Switzerland regulates crypto businesses rather than banning them: the [DLT Act](/posts/dlt-act-switzerland-explained/) adapted federal law to blockchain-based assets, and any business that handles crypto for clients sits inside the AMLA and SRO system described above. What is not allowed is offering those services commercially without the right membership or licence.

## What Is the Swiss Equivalent of the FCA?

FINMA, the Swiss Financial Market Supervisory Authority, is the closest counterpart to the UK's FCA: an independent federal authority that licenses and supervises banks, insurers, securities firms and FinTech licence holders. The difference that matters for crypto is that FINMA does not supervise SRO members directly. According to [FINMA's SRO page](https://www.finma.ch/en/authorisation/self-regulatory-organisations-sros/), those intermediaries are supervised by their SRO, and FINMA supervises the SROs. That two-tier structure is exactly what the crypto-institution reform would change for custodians and exchanges.

## How Much Does It Cost to Register a Company in Switzerland?

The largest fixed cost is share capital. A GmbH (limited liability company) needs at least CHF 20,000, which, as the [canton of Zurich's business portal](https://www.zh.ch/en/wirtschaft-arbeit/unternehmensportal/opu/firma-gruenden/rechtsform-waehlen/gmbh-gruenden.html) notes, must be paid in full at incorporation into a blocked account, and the formation must be notarised. A company limited by shares (AG) needs CHF 100,000 of share capital under the [Code of Obligations](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/27/317_321_377/en), of which at least CHF 50,000 has to be paid in. Notary and commercial register fees come on top and vary by canton; the official Zurich page does not state them. For a crypto firm, add the SRO admission fee and the cost of an AML officer before the first client is onboarded.

## What I Am Watching

The consultation closed on February 6, 2026, and the responses are now in. The Federal Council is working through them before it drafts the dispatch to parliament — the formal message that turns a consultation draft into an actual bill. As of November 2026 I could not find a published dispatch, which pushes the realistic window further into 2027 and makes an entry into force before then implausible. Until that changes, the SRO route above is the one that applies.

One response is worth singling out, because it aims straight at a design choice I flagged earlier. On February 11, 2026, the [Swiss Bankers Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_Bankers_Association) came out against the requirement that banks spin off a separate subsidiary to issue CHF stablecoins. Its argument: banks already sit at the center of the payment system and are held to stricter supervision than either new license category, so routing them through a second entity — what the association [framed as an unnecessary "licensing cascade"](https://cryptovalleyjournal.com/focus/legal-and-compliance/swiss-bankers-association-demands-direct-stablecoin-issuance-rights-for-banks/) — makes little sense to them. Whether the Federal Council holds the subsidiary line or bends to the banks is now one of the more concrete things to watch. That the banks pushed back this early tells you the stablecoin provisions are the part of this reform with real commercial stakes attached.

The SRO-supervised firms still carry the most structural risk. They know direct FINMA oversight is coming; the open questions are how much runway the transition rules give them and where the capital requirements land. The stablecoin segment, meanwhile, could move quickly once the law is finalized — several fintech players have been waiting for exactly this kind of legal clarity before building CHF-pegged products.

I will update this note as the dispatch and the parliamentary debate take shape.
